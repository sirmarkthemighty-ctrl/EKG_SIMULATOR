Waveforms are generated from a single moving-dipole model. Each region of myocardium contributes a vector of fixed direction and mass at the moment the conduction system reaches it; those vectors are summed into an instantaneous heart vector, and every lead is read off that vector through the electrodes that record it. Each chest electrode is placed on a thoracic ellipse by its own landmark and sees the dipole as a point source in a uniform medium, falling away with the square of the distance; the limb electrodes keep Einthoven’s equidistant triangle, because a limb conducts its root out to the wrist. Leads are then formed as the machine forms them, as differences of electrode potentials against a Wilson central terminal, so R wave progression and the hexaxial relations are consequences of that geometry rather than settings. The ventricle has one activation sequence: cut a branch of the conduction system and the region it fed is reached late, by slow spread from a neighbour, which is where the bundle branch and fascicular patterns come from rather than from stored templates. Every wall is held as two layers. The wavefront crosses each one outward, from the endocardium the Purkinje fibres land on; the epicardial action potential is the shorter, so recovery crosses back inward. Two reversals cancel, and the normal T wave points with the QRS — until an abnormal activation destroys that order, when it turns against it. The AV node is a tissue with a refractory period and a conduction curve, so Wenckebach, 2:1 block and the irregularity of atrial fibrillation are consequences rather than scripts, and re-entrant rhythms are circuits with an excitable gap, so a nodal blocker stops the ones that use the node as a limb and leaves the rest running. Injury is a property of the muscle rather than a shape on the paper. Each layer carries where it rests, how deep its phase one notch is, whether it holds its plateau and how long it lasts, and the J wave, the ST segment and the T wave are one curve: the difference between the two layers of every wall, read against the resting difference, because the TQ segment is the baseline the recorder restores. That subtraction is why a subepicardial injury elevates and a subendocardial one depresses, why the reciprocal change appears on its own in the leads facing away, why a fresh occlusion gives a hyperacute T and an older one a deep inversion, and why a change spread evenly over both layers shifts the T wave without displacing the ST segment at all. An occlusion names a vessel, inflammation names a layer, and a lost epicardial dome gives the coved pattern and its inverted T without either being drawn. Scar is modelled by silencing a wall, which is where the Q waves come from. Nothing here is a stored tracing, which is why changing the sequence changes every lead coherently, the right-sided and posterior ones included.

The treatments resolve to a single deterministic outcome so that the mechanism is legible. Real life is probabilistic: a first shock converts ventricular fibrillation only most of the time, adenosine sometimes fails at 6 mg, and atropine occasionally works where the model says it should not. Take the response here as the teaching point about mechanism, never as an expected success rate.

This is a teaching caricature. Intervals, amplitudes and morphologies are chosen to be recognisable, not to reproduce any individual patient, and must not be used to measure, calibrate or interpret a real recording.
